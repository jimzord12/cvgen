# CV format for junior and non-senior roles

Checked: 2026-11-03. Recheck after: 2027-05-03.
Question: What do recruiters and hiring managers want from a CV for ordinary, non-senior, not highly paid roles, and how should a one-page, scannability-first designed CV with a light custom touch look?
Review: research-reviewer, 3 rounds: FINDINGS, FINDINGS, PASS (2026-11-03); reports in `archive/work-records/research-junior-cv-format/reviews/`

## Bottom line

For entry-level, hourly, service, tourism, trades, junior office and junior software roles, the evidence supports **one full, dense page** with a conventional, strongly ordered hierarchy: name, then each role's title, employer and dates, where the eye lands in the first seconds. **No evidence supports self-rated skill bars**; nearly all practitioners reject them, one vendor still allows dots or bars in limited cases (claim 20), and parsers drop them. Heavy graphics, photos and "creative" layouts carry measurable costs. The one controlled experiment found that a formal layout almost doubled interview chances over a creative layout with the same content [S6]. In a 2016 survey, even creative-industry executives preferred traditional formats by 78% to 3% for infographics [S42]. What the evidence penalises is clutter and a hidden hierarchy, not design as such. A restrained personal touch (one accent colour, a crafted name block, a legible text face) has no direct test, but nothing found opposes it, and it agrees with every hierarchy finding. The client feedback "beautiful but not practical" matches the evidence closely. The base is weak, though. Almost all quantitative sources are **vendors who sell résumé services**, most famous statistics in this field cannot be traced, and no study separates hourly or service roles from "entry-level".

How to read the labels: **study** (described method, n where known), **vendor study** or **vendor survey** (run by a company that sells CV or recruiting services), **survey** (independent or staffing-firm), **official guidance**, **opinion** (practitioner, coach or careers office). The date after each source ref is the source's own date. "Undated" means none was found.

## 1. One page or two: current norms by region

1. **US recruiters overwhelmingly recommend one page for the average applicant.** In a 2024 survey of 418 US hiring professionals, 92% recommended one page. Most placed two pages at 10 or more years of experience (10+ years 45%, 15+ 17%, 20+ 31%), and 84% advised against 1.1 to 1.5 page CVs [S11, Jan–Mar 2024, vendor survey, n=418]. The same respondents also said résumés are often too short (63%), and **89% said too little information is worse than too much** [S11]. A one-page CV has to be full, not thin.
2. **Outcome data at entry level favour the shortest CVs.** On Jobscan's platform, entry-level résumés in the shortest length quartile got interviews at 17.9%, against 2.8% for the longest. For mid-career candidates the second-shortest quartile did best (9.8%) and the shortest did worst (3.8%) [S12, 8 Sep 2026, vendor analysis; n, time window and quartile definitions not published]. Read this as directional only.
3. **The one simulation that favours two pages favoured them least at entry level.** In ResumeGo's simulation, recruiters chose two-page résumés 2.3x as often overall, but only **1.4x at entry level**, against 2.6x at mid level and 2.9x for managerial roles [S10, Oct–Nov 2018, vendor study, n=482 hiring professionals]. Reviewers spent 2 to 4 minutes per résumé, far longer than measured first screens, so the result says more about a careful final read than about screening [S13, Dec 2018, opinion]. ResumeGo itself calls it "only a simulation" [S10].
4. **Staff-level preference has also been reported as an even split.** An older Accountemps/Robert Half survey found 47% of managers preferred two pages for staff-level hires and 46% one page [reported second-hand via S11; date and n unverified; treat as weak].
5. **UK: two A4 sides is the stated ceiling, with one page widely advised for juniors.** TargetJobs sets two A4 pages as the maximum for a graduate CV and says employer instructions come first [S15, undated, opinion/careers guidance]. Claims that the National Careers Service recommends two sides could not be checked at source.
6. **EU/Europass: "two pages are enough in most cases"; three may be seen as too long** [S16, undated older official guidance]. Vendors criticise Europass output as long and cluttered (about 17 sections, often 2 to 3 pages) [S17, Novoresume updated 24 Feb 2026, vendor opinion]. Those vendors sell alternatives.
7. **Germany: about one page for pupils, students and career starters; one to two pages under five years' experience** [S18, undated, opinion].
8. **Greece: no more than two A4 pages; one page for graduates and entry-level candidates.** KPMG Greece (People Services) says the CV "should not exceed two A4 pages" and lists contact details as name, phone, email and LinkedIn only, with no address or date of birth [S19, 1 Apr 2025, opinion from a recruiter/adviser]. A Greek CV-tool vendor recommends one A4 page for graduate and entry-level CVs [S20, undated, vendor opinion]. Guidance from the Greek job boards (kariera.gr, skywalker.gr, jobfind.gr) could not be retrieved.
9. **Junior software: one page is the US norm; two pages are more common in Europe.** A hiring manager and former engineering manager says one page is the norm in most of the US for developers with less than a few years' experience, and two pages are common in Europe. If a CV runs longer, everything relevant must be on page one [S14, 25 Nov 2020, opinion]. A UK bootcamp: "2 sides, maximum. 1 page? Even better!" [S53, undated, opinion].

**Gap.** No independent or peer-reviewed study of CV length by level exists in the notes, and none separates hourly, service, tourism or trades roles.

## 2. How recruiters scan, and what helps or hurts

10. **First screens last seconds. The famous "6 seconds" is a vendor figure for a first glance, not a full read.** TheLadders' 2012 eye-tracking study (30 recruiters over 10 weeks) measured about 6 seconds to the initial fit/no-fit decision, against 4 to 5 minutes in recruiters' own estimates [S1, 2012, vendor study, n=30]. The 2018 update reported 7.4 seconds [S2, 8 Nov 2018, vendor study]. The 2018 report publishes no headcount, recruiter experience, role types or résumé lengths, and critics call the figure a "vacuum metric" that varies by role [S3, 20 Oct 2020, opinion; S4, updated Feb 2025, opinion]. TheLadders sells résumé rewriting, and the 2012 report ends by promoting that service [S1]. **Trace:** the 6 s and 7.4 s figures do come from these reports, but they rest on a small sample and a thinly described method. Use them as "seconds", never as a precise number.
11. **Screening has stages.** In a 2024 survey, most US hiring professionals reported a first screen of 10 seconds to 1 minute (10–30 s: 33%; 30 s–1 min: 47%). 77% rated "easy to skim" at 7 to 10 out of 10 in importance [S9, Jan–Mar 2024, vendor survey, n=418]. Recruiters interviewed by StandOut CV describe an 8 to 10 second glance, 1 to 2 minutes to shortlist and 5 to 10 minutes on shortlisted CVs [S4, opinion]. Self-reports run consistently longer than measured times [S1].
12. **About 80% of first-screen gaze went to six fields:** name; current title and company; current start and end dates; previous title and company; previous dates; education. Beyond those, recruiters "did little more than scan for keywords", and detailed copy "became filler" in the initial decision [S1, 2012, vendor study, n=30]. In 2018, layouts with bold titles did better [S2].
13. **Time on Experience and Education predicts advancement.** In a peer-reviewed eye-tracking study, a classifier trained on recruiters' gaze alone predicted whether a résumé would be advanced (AUC 0.767). The strongest predictors were looking away from the page (taken as reflection), total viewing time and time on Experience and Education. The authors advise "clear, concise" résumés [S5, 2023, study; n not verifiable, the publisher page returned 403].
14. **Formal layout beat "creative" layout with identical content: almost twice the interview chance.** Twelve real candidates' content was set in three versions (formal on white, pastel paper, "creative" design) and rated by 45 recruitment professionals and 45 students. Formal did best and creative worst [S6, 2010 study, reported 8 Nov 2011; n=90 raters]. The UK Behavioural Insights Team reportedly cites the same finding [S7, 2021, research report; the PDF could not be parsed, so this citation is unverified]. This is the only controlled layout experiment in the notes. It tests convention against novelty and does not show that a well-structured distinctive design hurts.
15. **Features that help (consistent across sources):** simple layouts with clear section headings; bold titles; E- or F-pattern organisation; bulleted accomplishments; white space [S2, vendor study]; an obvious hierarchy with "less data" and even formatting, which raised usability ratings from 3.9 to 6.2 out of 7 [S1, vendor study]; headings visibly stronger than body text, front-loaded informative words, bold key terms, cut content [S8, 2017 reviewed Aug 2026, study of web reading, not CVs].
16. **Features that hurt:** clutter, long sentences, "multiple columns", very little white space, missing section or job headers [S2, vendor study]. In that study multiple columns were listed as part of a cluttered pattern; no source isolates a two-column layout as the cause. Poorly organised résumés produced erratic gaze and high cognitive load [S1]. On LinkedIn profiles (not CVs), the profile photo took 19% of fixation time, and pictures "reduced recruiters' analytical capability" [S1, vendor study]. In web reading, content on the right of a line or page is more often missed in an F-scan [S8, web, not CVs].
17. **Hard-to-skim CVs are common.** 41% of hiring professionals said at least 41% of the résumés they receive are hard to skim [S9, vendor survey, n=418].

**Gap.** No controlled study isolates one column against two, sidebar position, icons, font size or accent colour on CVs.

## 3. Graphics, photos, columns and skill bars: recruiters and ATS

### Skill bars and self-rated proficiency

18. **No experiment tests skill bars. Practitioner and vendor opinion against them is near-unanimous.** A hiring manager advises "Don't bother specifying your expertise level", since anything listed is assumed to be known well enough to be listed [S14, 2020, opinion]. Ask a Manager advises against skill ratings [S22, 2012, opinion]. An early argument (Danny Rubin) that dot ratings are meaningless self-assessments [S25, Feb 2015, opinion]. Hiration gives the reasons in full: there is "no scale underneath the number", an ATS "reads plain text" and may skip the bar, the graphic can leave a "mental note that you are early in your career", an 80% bar "quietly announces you are 20% short", and the bars take space that could hold evidence [S23, undated, vendor opinion]. Hiredly ("Stop rating your skills") [S64, 27 Nov 2023, updated 13 May 2026, vendor opinion; body text not retrievable] and Taro's software-engineer course, which covers why self-ratings "don't add any value" and their dangers "particularly with more junior engineers" [S65, undated, opinion], take the same line.
19. **Parsers drop the graphic and keep at most the skill name.** Jobscan says bar graphs without numbers are a poor choice because "the ATS can't read the levels". It recommends words instead: grouped levels, or a level in parentheses [S24, 14 Nov 2024, vendor claim, no test data].
20. **Minority dissent:** Resume Genius allows dots or bars for creative or tech roles or tight space, while advising against star or percentage self-ratings [S27, updated 10 Jun 2026, vendor opinion].
21. **Languages have a recognised text scale: CEFR A1–C2**, which Europass uses for self-assessment [S26, undated, official guidance]. It is machine-readable as text and familiar to European employers.
22. **"Years per tool" tables:** no source addressed them directly (gap).

### Photos

23. **Photos bring in judgements unrelated to the job, with large measured effects.** Israel: 5,312 CVs sent in pairs to 2,656 openings. Attractive men got 19.9% callbacks, plain men 13.7%, men with no photo 9.2%. For women the effect reversed: women with no photo were called back most often [S28, Management Science 61(8), Aug 2015, study, n=5,312 CVs; the callback rates come from coverage of the 2010 working paper, 28 Nov 2010, as the journal page could not be fetched]. Germany: identical qualifications got 18.8% callbacks with a German name, 13.5% with a Turkish name and 4.2% with a Turkish name and a headscarf in the photo [S29, 2016, study, correspondence test]. A methodological review confirms that photos carry traits beyond the one being tested [S30, 2018, study/review].
24. **Norms by market.** No photo in the US, UK and Canada, linked to the UK Equality Act 2010 and US EEO practice. Still customary in Germany [S33, undated, staffing-firm guidance; vendor guides in the notes]. **Greece is contested.** A Greek press article advises leaving the photo off unless the ad asks for one [S21, undated, opinion]. KPMG Greece's contact list does not include one [S19]. General European guidance that a photo is "common" in Greece rests on no Greek source. No Greek employer data exist in the notes.
25. **Unsupported statistic: "88% of recruiters reject a resume with a photo."** It is attributed to a NetCredit infographic, and no primary survey or n could be found [repeated in S32]. Do not use it. A self-selected LinkedIn poll found 81% saying "No" to photos [S31, Jun 2023, poll, n not reported].

### ATS parsing

26. **Unsupported statistic: "75% of résumés are rejected by ATS before a human sees them."** It is widely attributed to marketing by Preptel, a résumé-optimisation firm that closed around 2013; no primary Preptel source or date was located. No study, survey or dataset supports it [S39, undated, opinion/trace; S37, vendor]. Separately, in 25 structured interviews, 23 US recruiters (92%) said their ATS does not auto-reject on formatting, design or content. Knockout questions (work authorisation, licence, location) are the real automatic filter [S34, Sep–Oct 2025, vendor interviews, n=25, not representative].
27. **A failed parse does not hide the CV from humans.** In Greenhouse, an unsuccessful parse means fields are not auto-filled. The recruiter enters them by hand, and the résumé stays attached and visible [S35, updated 2 Mar 2026, official documentation].
28. **Columns are an official parse risk in Greenhouse, but a failed parse means manual entry, not rejection.** Greenhouse lists columns among the layouts that cause unsuccessful parses (claim 29) [S35]. Parser vendors are working on the problem: Textkernel detects column separators by scoring each whitespace gap with a gradient-boosting model; a separate neural classifier selected column-heavy training documents. It estimates columned CVs at about 10–15% or more of documents (the page gives both "10–15%" and "at least 15%") and that its earlier rules missed some separators [S36, undated, vendor technical blog].
29. **Real parsing risks are specific, and Greenhouse documents them.** Greenhouse's official list of parse-failure causes: files over 2.5 MB (often from high-quality images); photos, images or word art; résumés uploaded as images; tables, headers, footers, columns or inconsistently formatted sections; name or contact details in a header, footer or text box; letters separated by spaces; abbreviated job titles; placeholder-looking data [S35, updated 2 Mar 2026, official documentation]. Third-party guides add that non-standard fonts can garble characters [S37, undated, vendor claim]. **Claims about Workday, Oracle Taleo or iCIMS specifically are not confirmed by those vendors.** Even a third-party Workday guide says Workday does not publish its parser internals [S38, 17 Oct 2026, vendor]. A practical self-test: copy all the text from the PDF into a plain-text editor and check what survives [S55, undated, opinion].
30. **Many small employers have no ATS.** 57% of "growing" and 40% of non-growing US small businesses use applicant tracking software [S40, 2023, vendor survey, n>2,000]. Most small companies rely on email, spreadsheets and paper [S41, undated, analyst opinion]. An often-repeated "70% of large vs 20% of SMBs" has no traceable origin [S62, unsourced]. No Greek or EU SME data were found.

### Infographics, icons and heavy design

31. **Infographic CVs collapsed even in creative hiring.** US advertising and marketing executives hiring for creative roles preferred traditional Word or PDF résumés (78%, up from 70% in 2013) over infographics (3%, down from 20%). The spokesperson said a visually unique résumé may "pique an employer's interest" but must "above all else be easy to read, concise", and advised against "too many charts and colours" [S42, 5 May 2016, staffing-firm survey via independent research firm, n>400].
32. **20% of interviewed recruiters called over-designed résumés a turn-off** (heavy graphics, decorative fonts, photos) because they are harder to scan [S34, 2025, vendor interviews, n=25].
33. **Icons:** no direct evidence. Parsers ignore images, and some fail on unexpected image data [S37, vendor claim]. The consensus is that icons are neutral to humans only when the real text (number, email, URL) sits beside them.

## 4. What belongs on the CV and what waits for the interview

34. **The CV says what the candidate can do and proves it; the interview tests how well.** Recommended: name technologies or tools inside experience or project bullets rather than only in a skills block; write specific, quantified, active statements rather than duties; keep a master CV and tailor each version by moving the relevant experience up [S14, 2020, opinion]. Make small profile adjustments for each posting [S19, 2025, opinion]. List only the skills the role needs, because extra skills crowd a single page and look untailored [S52, undated, vendor opinion].
35. **Leave off at junior level (UK, US, international):** photo and date of birth (anti-discrimination norms) [S33, undated, staffing-firm guidance]; hobbies unless they show relevant skills [S54, undated, official employer guidance]; interests last, as conversation starters for the interview [S14]; "references available on request", no longer standard [S63, undated, staffing-firm guidance]; the full street address, since Greek guidance lists only name, phone, email and LinkedIn [S19]. Germany differs on photo and date of birth [S18; S33].
36. **Hospitality and entry-level guidance points to one page** [S50, undated, opinion] and a clean layout [S49, 22 Jul 2022, opinion].
37. **Gaps:** no study or survey with n on tailoring effects, bullet length or bullet count at junior or hourly level. No official careers-service guidance specific to hospitality, tourism or trades CVs was retrieved.

## 5. Distinctive without hurting scannability

38. **Colour: vendor surveys conflict.** Canva reports 62% of hiring managers prefer colour to black and white, and 38% of dissatisfied managers found résumés "boring". In the same survey, 48% avoid résumés with too much text and 45% skip disorganised ones [S43, reported 17 Jan 2025, vendor survey, n=4,200 across 10 countries, method not published]. An earlier Canva write-up says 57% prefer visual elements [S44, 27 Feb 2024, vendor survey, n "over 10,000" including job seekers]. ResumeLab, on the other side, reportedly says 72% prefer (73% in the AOL syndication) black and white and 71% prefer formal over 7% creative, noting "it depends on the kind of job" [S45, undated, vendor survey, n and method not found, seen only via search snippets]. Canva sells visual templates.
39. **Typeface must fit the genre and read as legible.** In an experiment on onscreen documents including résumés, typefaces judged inappropriate or neutral lowered the perceived character of the author. The appropriate résumé faces were those high in perceived legibility (serif or sans), not display faces [S46, 2007, study; n behind paywall]. Related work found that less congruent typefaces made documents perceived more negatively [S47, 2007, study].
40. **Minimum readable size: body text should stay at or above about 9–10 pt.** Vision science puts the critical print size at about 0.2° x-height, roughly a 9 pt body in Times New Roman at normal reading distance. Reanalysed Tinker data show fastest reading at 11 pt and a significant decline at 8 pt. The effective size depends on each face's x-height [S48, 2011, study, peer-reviewed review]. Preferred line lengths are about 45–72 characters, while longer screen lines read faster but are liked less [S58, 1998–2004 studies via secondary summary].
41. **Hospitality tolerates a "toned down" creative touch.** A university hospitality coach recommends a layout that is "eye catching, clean, sophisticated and clear", with one or two standout features. Their sample shows a colour-blocked sidebar or summary [S49, 22 Jul 2022, opinion].
42. **Memorability is unstudied.** No audit or field study varies only visual design and measures callbacks or recall. Claims that distinctive design raises callbacks are opinion or vendor marketing [notes: none found]. A student eye-tracking poster found that stylised headings changed gaze patterns, not outcomes [S61, 2020, pilot, no n, qualitative retention differences only].
43. **Unsupported statistics:** a "University of Iowa 2018" serif vs sans study, "Ladders 2022: 60% dismiss non-standard fonts", an "HBR 23%" figure and "creative layouts are shortlisted at half the rate". No primary source was found for any of them, so do not cite them. The Arnulf experiment [S6] is the traceable source for a similar "about twice" effect and should be cited instead.

## 6. Does the right design differ by seniority, pay or industry?

44. **No survey or study breaks design preference down by seniority, pay level or hourly against salaried roles** (gap).
45. **Industry differences are small, and conservative everywhere.** Even creative-industry hiring preferred traditional formats in a 2016 survey [S42]. Junior software guides push hardest toward one column, standard 10–12 pt fonts and no tables, icons or images [S51, 2024–2026 undated, opinion; S14 recommends a single column]. Hospitality allows one or two restrained standout features [S49]. No sector guidance for trades, maritime or finance was retrieved. The nearest data point is ResumeLab's unverified "depends on the kind of job" [S45].
46. **Premium services tier by seniority and sell content, not design.** Quoted tiers run about $159 entry, $259 professional and $359 executive, or $200–500 entry against $1,000–3,500+ executive. Executive work is sold on "strategy and branding" [S56, undated, vendor or marketplace data]. Design is pitched as "modern but ATS-friendly", a claim no one has verified independently.

## Implications for a one-page, scannability-first CV type with a light custom touch

These are for CVgen. Each line says whether the evidence supports it or whether it is inference or opinion.

**Supported by evidence (vendor-heavy, but consistent across sources):**

- **One full page for entry-level and junior candidates, with no stub second page.** Supported by claims 1–3 and 5–9. Fill the page; a thin page is its own failure (claim 1). Extending this to hourly workers is inference: no source studies hourly roles separately.
- **Put the six first-scan fields in fixed, prominent positions:** name, then for each role the title, employer and dates, then education. Job titles should be the strongest line in each entry (claims 12, 13, 15). Anchoring them on the left is inference from web-reading data (claim 16, S8), not tested on CVs.
- **Clear headings, white space and bullets. No clutter and no "creative" layout that hides the expected fields** (claims 14–16, 31).
- **No self-rated skill bars, dots or percentages.** Skills as plain text, proven in bullets. Languages as CEFR levels or named certificates (claims 18–21). This matches the client feedback directly.
- **No photo by default for UK, US and international applications.** Treat Germany as an exception and Greece as undecided (claims 23–25).
- **All content as real text in a logical reading order. Contact details in the body, not a page header or footer. No text inside images. Check by copy-pasting the PDF text** (claims 27–29). Auto-rejection for design is largely a myth (claim 26), but lost text is a real risk.
- **Body text no smaller than about 9–10 pt for a normal x-height face** (claim 40). This agrees with CVgen's no-font-shrinking rule.

**Inference (reasoned from the evidence, not tested directly):**

- Put the personal touch where the eye already goes: a crafted name block or header, one accent colour that marks the scan path (titles, rules), and a legible text face with character. Keep it out of the body (claims 15, 38, 39, 41). Neither colour camp rejects a single restrained accent.
- A narrow sidebar is acceptable only for secondary facts (contact, languages, licences, certificates), never for the career story, because right-side content risks being skipped and "multiple columns" sits inside the clutter pattern (claims 16, 28). A single column is the safer default for junior software (claim 45).
- Role illustrations or motifs must be clearly decorative, small, outside the reading path and never carry information (claims 16, 32, 33). One motif at most for a scannability-first type.
- For hourly, tourism and trades work, the first-scan zone should also hold role-critical facts: licences and certificates, availability or seasons, languages, city or region (claims 12, 26 on knockout filters). Many of these employers have no ATS and read the PDF or a printout, so visual hierarchy matters more than parsing for them (claim 30).

**Opinion (the report's judgement, not evidence):**

- "Premium" for this tier should mean craft in typography, spacing and a quiet signature in the header, not more pictures. That fits a market gap: services sell executive content, not design-led junior CVs (claim 46). Whether a crafted but restrained design actually improves callbacks is untested (claim 42).
