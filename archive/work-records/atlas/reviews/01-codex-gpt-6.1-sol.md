**FINDINGS**

Typography, spacing and theme consistency are strong; the main weaknesses are process clarity, map usability and a visual identity that remains too close to a conventional dashboard. Interaction behavior cannot be verified from these screenshots.

1. **Blocking — Design run: Map, Walk through, drawer; index.** Optional browsing appears to be a required gate. The drawer explicitly says the owner’s look is “Optional and any time,” including before or after the gates. Yet the map connects step 4 → owner’s look → research gate, and the index puts browsing under work that “cannot move until you act.” Move optional browsing beside the main process, with connections to the stages where it is available. Reserve “Waiting on you” for required decisions; show an explicit owner-requested pause separately if that explains the current hold.

2. **Material — All flow Maps, especially client journey on mobile.** The current step is not reliably visible. On the mobile client map, the actor column consumes roughly half the available width, Sign-off is fully visible, and the “You are here” card is mostly clipped at the right edge. Desktop maps also begin midway through their flows without an obvious indication of the omitted earlier steps. Add a persistent overview navigator showing the visible range and current position. On mobile, compact actor labels and position the current card fully inside the viewport.

3. **Material — All flow Maps.** Lane height is driven by equal treatment of every actor rather than useful activity. Multiple reviewer rows contain only a tiny participation ring, leaving hundreds of pixels of empty space and pushing other actors below the screenshot. Make primary action lanes prominent and supporting participation compact—either shorter lanes or helper badges on the relevant card. The whole mechanism should be easier to survey.

4. **Material — Flow Maps, both themes.** The main route is visually weaker than the badges and card borders. Thin, subdued connectors travel long distances between lanes; the gold bypass and blue loops attract more attention than the ordinary sequence. Strengthen the primary route’s contrast and arrowheads, separate participation lines from process connections, and give bypass destinations clearer labels. A reader should trace the normal path before decoding exceptions.

5. **Material — Mobile navigation and Walk through.** The header truncates navigation after “A…”, while the progress strip stops visibly at Text. The desktop step list—with its step names and “NOW” marker—disappears on mobile. This leaves later phases and the relationship between the selected step and today’s position poorly exposed. Use an explicit navigation menu and a compact step selector showing both “Viewing 1 of 20” and “Current: 13,” with a direct jump to the current step.

6. **Material — Flow pages, desktop and mobile.** Too much explanation precedes the interactive mechanism. Desktop diagrams start around 650–706 px down; mobile repeats the introduction, three summaries and a lengthy status card before reaching either view. Reduce the introduction to a short orientation sentence, compress the current-status card, and make the three summaries expandable. Bring the map or selected step substantially higher.

7. **Material — System map, both themes.** The initial state explains “what lives where” but barely communicates “how they feed each other.” Equal-sized area containers read as a directory of cards; relationships require discovering and hovering individual parts. Show a small default backbone—facts → template/framework → render → revision → approval/export—with secondary links revealed on interaction. Keep the existing inventory available beneath or alongside it.

8. **Material — Overall visual direction, especially index and roster.** The execution is polished, but repeated rounded cards, pills, muted surfaces and three-column grids produce a familiar dashboard appearance. The compass is the strongest distinctive element, yet it does little beyond decoration. Develop a recognizable visual language around routes, handoffs and decision gates, and carry it through page previews and diagrams. The index cards should preview each mechanism visually instead of repeating three paragraphs of summary.

9. **Minor — Drawers.** Short details leave most of the full-height panel empty, particularly Change review and Client envelopes. The footer controls sit far from the content, making their relationship to the selected step weak. Place previous/next controls beside the step heading, with destination names, and keep the walkthrough action near the details. A shorter content panel could also preserve more map context.

10. **Minor — Dark theme secondary text; mobile sources and footer.** Source paths, timestamps, model annotations and instructional captions are frequently tiny and low contrast. The many source chips also compete with useful content in walkthrough cards. Increase contrast for information needed to navigate or understand state, and fold file references into an expandable “Sources” control.

The two highest-impact changes are to **correct the optional-versus-required decision model** and **redesign map navigation and lane density so the current mechanism is visible at a glance, including on mobile**.
